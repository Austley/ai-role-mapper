# AI vs. Human Alignment Framework
### Built from 75 marketing + comms job descriptions (7 in depth; validation batches: 33 senior/mid, 15 entry-level, 20 creative/social/paid media)
*The canonical rulebook for the AI Role Mapper. Batch 1 breakdowns are below; later batch verdicts live in companion docs batch-2-role-assessments.md, batch-3-entry-level-assessments.md, and batch-4-creative-social-paid-assessments.md.*

---

## The three buckets

1. **AI can largely handle it.** The task is pattern-based, has an established format, or is monitoring/synthesis work. A human still spot-checks, but AI does 70%+ of the labor.
2. **AI assists, human drives.** AI speeds up drafting, research, or analysis, but the output only works because a human shaped the framing, verified accuracy, or made the call.
3. **Human required.** Trust, relationships, live judgment, accountability, or leadership. AI can prep you for these moments but cannot be in them.

Time-share estimates below are my rough judgment calls for a typical week in each role, not sourced stats. They exist so you can react to them.

## Role 1: Director, PR & Media Strategy (American Clean Power Association)

**Role in one line:** Press secretary for a policy advocacy org. Rapid response, reporter relationships, executive counsel.

### AI can largely handle
- Media monitoring, coverage tracking, and news cycle alerts
- Coverage summaries, leadership updates, and post-engagement reports
- First drafts of routine press materials with established formats (media advisories, background docs, boilerplate sections of releases)
- Building and maintaining media lists and briefing books
- Formatting and distribution logistics

### AI assists, human drives
- Press releases, statements, talking points, and op-eds (AI drafts, human owns framing and accuracy, especially on policy)
- Translating complex policy/regulatory developments into accessible messaging (AI synthesizes, human verifies and frames because a wrong nuance here is a credibility problem)
- Proactive media strategy development (AI researches angles and reporters, human picks the play)
- Interview prep materials and message framing docs
- Identifying risks and opportunities in emerging narratives (AI flags patterns, human judges what actually matters)

### Human required
- Serving as the press-facing spokesperson and primary reporter contact
- Building and maintaining reporter, editor, and producer relationships
- Rapid-response judgment calls under deadline (what to say, what not to say, when to say nothing)
- Real-time media counsel to executives during interviews and breaking news
- Managing PR agency relationships
- After-hours crisis availability (though AI monitoring shrinks the always-on burden)

**Rough time-share:** ~20% AI handles / ~40% AI assists / ~40% human required.
**The insight:** The writing in this job looks automatable but mostly isn't safe to automate, because every word carries the org's credibility with reporters. What AI genuinely takes off this person's plate is the monitoring and reporting grind, which is real hours.

## Role 2: Internal Communications Director (CGI)

**Role in one line:** Senior internal comms lead for a huge matrixed org. Narrative coherence, channel governance, community building.

### AI can largely handle
- Content calendar management and planning rhythm upkeep
- Intake, review-routing, and distribution workflows
- Newsletter drafting, formatting, and channel publishing (Teams posts, mailbox comms)
- Brand/standards consistency checks across communications
- KPI monitoring and engagement dashboards
- Synthesizing employee feedback and survey data into themes

### AI assists, human drives
- Weaving Global, U.S., HR, and BU inputs into one coherent narrative (AI holds the threads, human decides the story)
- Behavior-change and adoption communications (AI drafts, human knows what will actually move people)
- Event comms plans for Town Halls and internal events
- Shaping partner stories once they're surfaced
- Localization of global initiatives

### Human required
- Convening and leading a community of comms leads who don't report to you (pure influence-without-authority)
- Consultative partnering: sitting with a stakeholder to clarify purpose and audience before anything gets written
- Surfacing authentic employee stories (people tell real stories to people they trust)
- Leading and developing the team of comms consultants
- Representing the SBU in the global comms community
- Executive presence in the room during events and leadership moments

**Rough time-share:** ~30% AI handles / ~30% AI assists / ~40% human required.
**The insight:** This is the most operationally automatable role of the seven on the channel side. But its actual center of gravity is trust across a matrix, and no workflow tool builds that.

## Role 3: Director, Global Enterprise Communications (Capital One)

**Role in one line:** Strategic counselor to senior execs at a regulated financial giant. Short JD, heavy on judgment.

### AI can largely handle
- Audience sentiment analysis and communications performance measurement
- Trend monitoring across financial services, tech, and comms
- Campaign reporting and narrative impact dashboards

### AI assists, human drives
- Designing 360-degree campaigns (AI generates options and drafts assets, human aligns them to LOB imperatives and enterprise narrative)
- Turning data into a point of view worth bringing to executives

### Human required
- Being a trusted strategic counselor to senior executives (the entire premise of the role)
- Leading, coaching, and developing the team
- Navigating a highly regulated environment where a comms misstep has legal weight
- Cross-functional leadership across complex corporate functions

**Rough time-share:** ~15% AI handles / ~30% AI assists / ~55% human required.
**The insight:** This JD barely lists tasks because the job isn't tasks. It's judgment and trust in a regulated environment. The least automatable role in the set.

## Role 4: Head of Marketing (Galvanick, OT cybersecurity startup)

**Role in one line:** First marketing hire, explicitly told to build with "an aggressive orientation toward AI-powered automation."

### AI can largely handle
- Lead scoring and automated multi-channel lead gen execution
- Nurture campaigns that adapt to engagement patterns (once a human designs them)
- Content repurposing at scale: one CTO whiteboard session becomes briefs, blogs, social, video scripts
- Account intelligence for sales
- GEO/AI-discoverability content structuring
- Competitive monitoring and reporting

### AI assists, human drives
- Technical content creation (AI drafts, human verifies with engineering because wrong technical claims kill credibility with security buyers)
- Persona messaging for Operations Leaders vs. Security Execs vs. Technical Evaluators
- Sales enablement materials
- Event and campaign strategy

### Human required
- Sitting with the CTO and absorbing the whiteboard session (extraction is human; scaling it is AI)
- Analyst, media, and practitioner relationships
- Customer advocacy: getting real customers to publicly vouch for you
- Deciding the brand strategy and category positioning
- Architecting the AI systems themselves

**Rough time-share:** ~40% AI handles / ~30% AI assists / ~30% human required.
**The insight:** This JD is the future arriving early. The execution layer is explicitly assigned to AI, and the human's job is trust (customers, analysts, the CTO's knowledge) plus system design. Notice the new skill hiding in here: architecting AI workflows is itself a human-required task.

## Role 5: Marketing Leader, Flow Studio (Autodesk)

**Role in one line:** Zero-to-one consumer marketing leader building a new AI/creativity category. Creator-led growth.

### AI can largely handle
- Performance tracking across awareness, engagement, conversion, and usage
- Testing/iteration mechanics (variant generation, reporting on what worked)
- Content adaptation across channels once the creative direction is set

### AI assists, human drives
- Translating AI product capabilities into consumer narratives
- Campaign concepts and creative development
- Defining where content shows up

### Human required
- Creator and community relationships (creators smell inauthenticity instantly, and community is built on reciprocity)
- Prioritizing what gets content and what doesn't (pure judgment with real tradeoffs)
- Deep partnership with product and creative teams
- Setting the marketing operating model
- Cultural relevance instincts: knowing what will land as cool vs. cringe

**Rough time-share:** ~25% AI handles / ~30% AI assists / ~45% human required.
**The insight:** Consumer and creator marketing is more human-heavy than B2B demand gen, because the product being sold is cultural credibility. AI can't be cool on your behalf.

## Role 6: Head of Marketing Operations (Render)

**Role in one line:** Build the AI-first ops engine for a lean team serving 6M developers. Explicitly "an AI-first role."

### AI can largely handle
- Lifecycle marketing execution triggered by product behavior
- Outbound infrastructure operations and deliverability management
- Attribution reporting and analytics dashboards
- High-frequency task automation across the stack
- Data hygiene across a database of millions

### AI assists, human drives
- Building the attribution models (AI does the math, human decides what "counts" and why, which is a political and strategic question)
- Board update and business review prep
- AI enablement/training for the rest of the team

### Human required
- Architecting the stack: which systems, which workflows, what's the system of record
- Being the connective tissue between Marketing, Sales, and Finance (alignment between orgs is negotiation, not automation)
- Judgment about what should NOT be automated
- Evangelizing AI adoption (behavior change in humans is a human job)

**Rough time-share:** ~50% AI handles / ~25% AI assists / ~25% human required.
**The insight:** The most automatable role in the set, by design. But notice the paradox: the job exists BECAUSE of AI, and the human value is entirely in architecture, judgment, and cross-team trust. This role didn't shrink from AI. It was created by it.

## Role 7: Marketing Leader (Fairmarkit, procurement SaaS)

**Role in one line:** Player-coach full-stack marketing leader reporting to the CEO. Pipeline ownership plus brand.

### AI can largely handle
- ABM campaign execution across channels
- Funnel and conversion reporting
- Competitive intel gathering and monitoring
- GEO/AIO optimization mechanics
- Experiment tracking and performance analysis

### AI assists, human drives
- Positioning, messaging, and differentiation (AI generates options, human picks what lands with the ICP based on real customer contact)
- Campaign creative development
- Product launch narratives
- Budget allocation analysis (AI models it, human owns the tradeoffs)
- Funnel diagnosis with Sales and CS

### Human required
- Joining QBRs and sales calls to stay close to customers (the JD says this explicitly, and it's where positioning instincts actually come from)
- Coaching the team and setting the operating rhythm
- PR agency, analyst, and media relationships and briefings
- Making customers the heroes (getting a customer to star in your story is a relationship outcome)
- Leading from the front when the business needs hands

**Rough time-share:** ~30% AI handles / ~35% AI assists / ~35% human required.
**The insight:** "Player-coach" roles resist clean automation percentages because the human's presence in execution is partly the point: you can't coach work you never touch.

# Cross-cutting rules (what the tool will actually run on)

These are the patterns that held across all 7 JDs. This is the draft rulebook. React to these hardest, because they generalize.

## → AI can largely handle
1. **Monitoring and tracking.** Media coverage, sentiment, competitors, KPIs, funnels. AI watches tirelessly; humans can't and shouldn't.
2. **Routine summarizing.** Raw coverage digests, performance dashboards, meeting synthesis. Note: the strategic report that goes up the chain is NOT in this bucket anymore. See rule 13.
3. **First drafts of formatted content.** Anything with an established template: advisories, newsletters, briefing docs, nurture emails. (For early-career users, see the "keep your reps" flag in observation H.)
4. **Repurposing at scale.** One source asset becoming ten channel-specific versions.
5. **Workflow operations.** Intake, routing, scheduling, calendar management, distribution, list hygiene.
6. **Synthesis of large inputs.** Survey feedback, transcripts, engagement data, research piles.
7. **Personalization mechanics.** Adapting messages by persona/segment once a human defines the personas.
8. **In-platform advertising mechanics.** Campaign setup, trafficking, bid management, budget pacing, QA. Shifting faster than anything else in this bucket because the ad platforms themselves are the automators: smart bidding, dynamic creative, automated campaign management are being built into the products whether practitioners adopt AI or not. The career path moves up the stack to rule 20.

## → AI assists, human drives
9. **High-stakes writing.** Press releases, statements, op-eds, executive messaging. AI accelerates; a human owns every word because credibility is on the line.
10. **Translation of complexity.** Policy, technical, or regulatory content into plain language. AI is a strong translator and a confident hallucinator; human verification is non-negotiable.
11. **Strategy development.** AI widens the option set and stress-tests thinking. The choice is human.
12. **Analysis into decisions.** AI finds the pattern; the human decides what it means and what to do.
13. **Leadership-facing reporting.** Moved out of bucket one on purpose. The report you send leadership is how they perceive your function's value. What you surface, and how you frame a miss, is political work disguised as admin work. AI builds the data layer; the human owns the framing.
14. **Relationship upkeep.** AI tracks beat changes and job moves, maintains interaction history, times the follow-up, drafts the personalization. The human sends it and shows up. Upkeep is assistable; building is not (rule 21).
15. **Prep for human moments.** Briefing docs, interview prep, talking points. AI builds the prep; the moment itself is bucket three.
16. **Crisis support systems.** AI runs war-room simulations of potential scenarios, pre-drafts statements for each, checks that messaging ties back to mission/vision/values, and flags when a statement diverges from what a spokesperson said previously. AI catches the inconsistencies humans miss under pressure; the live call stays human (rule 22).
17. **Voice capture and ghostwriting.** Writing as a specific person: CEO speeches, executive LinkedIn, bylines. AI drafts, repurposes, and checks consistency against past statements, but authentic voice comes from sustained human proximity to the person. Warning the tool should surface: an executive presence that readers clock as AI-written damages the reputation it exists to build.
18. **Community management at scale.** AI triages the volume: routine responses, sentiment monitoring, around-the-clock coverage. Humans own the voice moments: the witty reply, the graceful save with an angry customer, knowing when a comment thread is becoming a crisis. AI handles volume; humans handle voice.
19. **Design and production craft.** Layouts, asset adaptation, motion graphics, video editing, template systems. Image and video generation is moving this category faster than any other in this bucket. Original concept and art direction belong to rule 29; production craft is becoming AI-accelerated execution.
20. **Measurement science.** Incrementality testing, MMM, attribution modeling, geo-experiments. AI does the math; humans design the experiment, choose which model to believe, and decide what "incremental" means. This is where paid media careers move as rule 8 automates from underneath.

## → Human required
21. **Relationship building.** Reporters, analysts, creators, customers, KOLs, Hill staff, platform reps, community members, executives. Trust transfers person to person. Zero exceptions across all 75 JDs assessed. AI can maintain the record of a relationship (rule 14); it cannot create one.
22. **Live judgment under pressure.** The crisis decision itself: what to say, what not to say, when silence is the move. An AI-equipped human (rule 16) makes this call faster and better informed, but makes it themselves.
23. **Being the accountable face.** Spokesperson duties, executive counsel, briefings you deliver in person. Someone's name and credibility is attached.
24. **Leading people.** Coaching, developing, convening, influencing without authority, driving behavior change.
25. **Prioritization with real tradeoffs.** What gets resourced, what gets killed, where budget goes.
26. **Extracting knowledge from humans.** The CTO whiteboard session, the customer story, the exec's real opinion. Getting it out of someone's head is human work; scaling it afterward is AI work.
27. **Vendor and agency management.** Briefing, negotiating, holding partners accountable, deciding to fire an agency. Appears in nearly every JD in the corpus. Tracking partner performance against deliverables is AI work; the accountability relationship is not.
28. **Advocacy sourcing.** Getting a customer, peer, or practitioner to publicly vouch for you: case study participation, advisory boards, speaking slots, reviews. Pure relationship work. Producing the asset afterward is heavily AI-assistable, so the tool should split advocacy into those two halves.
29. **Creative direction and taste.** Setting the quality bar, reviewing and approving work, killing what isn't good enough, guarding the brand. AI multiplies creative options at near-zero cost, which makes taste MORE valuable, not less: someone has to be the filter, and the filter is the job. A portfolio is evidence of accumulated judgment.
30. **Cultural judgment.** Deciding when and how a brand credibly participates in a cultural moment. AI surfaces the trend in seconds; whether your brand belongs in it is a human call with asymmetric downside. As one agency JD put it: knowing "when bold is brilliant and when bold is a screenshot waiting to happen."
31. **Architecting the AI systems themselves.** The newest human skill on the list, and now an explicit requirement across a large share of the corpus, from entry-level nice-to-haves to a C-suite mandate to run a generative AI hub.

# Context modifiers (applied after bucket assignment)

The same task lands in different buckets depending on context. The tool applies these after initial classification:

**M1. Regulated-industry modifier.** Litigation, healthcare, financial services, government, defense, policy. Tasks shift one bucket toward human: legal review, compliance sign-off, and consent requirements attach to work that would be routine elsewhere. A customer story at a SaaS startup is bucket one drafting; a patient story at a healthcare company is not.

**M2. Restricted-environment modifier.** Classified settings, security clearances, strict data policies. These can prohibit AI tools entirely regardless of how automatable a task looks. The tool must ask about environment (or detect signals like "clearance," "classified," "HIPAA," "litigation") or it will give actively wrong advice.

**M3. Physical-presence modifier.** Trade shows, on-site event execution, live briefing support, in-person counsel. Being there is human by definition. The shell around presence (logistics docs, run-of-show, signage specs, follow-up campaigns) is AI work. Split presence tasks into human core and automatable shell.

# Open observations for the tool's design

**A. Seniority changes a lot, but isn't the only driver.** Confirmed with mid-level data in batch 2: manager-level coordination and marcomm roles run ~45% "AI can largely handle" versus ~15-30% for directors and VPs. But the Zillow litigation comms role is a mid-level title with a senior-level human share (stakes and regulation matter as much as rank), and batch 4's Electra and Targeted Victory roles are senior titles with ~40-50% bucket one (ops-heavy function type overrides seniority). Career stage is a useful prior, not a verdict; function type and context modifiers finish the picture.

**A2. Paid media is the family the platforms are automating themselves.** Google and Meta are building smart bidding, dynamic creative, and automated campaign management into their own products. Ad ops automates from underneath regardless of practitioner choices. The career path in this family moves up the stack: from campaign mechanics to measurement science, budget strategy, and vendor accountability.

**B. The market has already moved.** Across all 40 JDs, explicitly requiring AI fluency is now the norm, with the strongest phrasing being BambooHR's "AI fluency through changed practice, not stated belief." AI-era discoverability (GEO/AEO, agent-mediated discovery) is a recurring brand-new job duty. "Will AI take this job" is the wrong question; the JDs are already asking "can you wield it."

**C. Bucket one tasks eat disproportionate hours.** Monitoring, reporting, and formatting are maybe 20-30% of the task list but often more of the actual week. The tool's real value message: AI gives you those hours back for bucket three work.

**D. The same task can live in different buckets depending on stakes.** A newsletter draft is bucket one. A crisis statement is bucket two at best. The tool needs stakes-awareness, not just task-matching.

**E. The direction of travel flag.** Every task verdict also gets a stability marker: stable (this bucket assignment should hold) or shifting (this task is drifting toward more automation). Examples: media monitoring is bucket one, stable. First drafts of routine materials are bucket one, still shifting as AI improves. Translation of technical complexity is bucket two, shifting, because verification tooling keeps getting better. Relationship building is bucket three, stable, full stop. This keeps the tool honest, warns people against getting comfortable in bucket two, and gives the tool a built-in reason to be updated (which is also an ongoing content engine: "here's what moved buckets this year").

**F. Entry-level is two tracks, not one.** Desk-track entry roles (agency associates, coordinators, marketing assistants) run 50-60% "AI can largely handle," the highest exposure in the corpus. Presence-track entry roles (field marketing, event coordination, multimedia craft) run as low as 5-15%, the lowest in the corpus, often at lower pay. AI exposure does not map onto pay or prestige: it maps onto how much of the job is structured desk work. The tool's early-career advice splits accordingly: desk-track people get the "keep your reps" message; presence-track people get "your moat is showing up; use AI for the paperwork around it."

**G. Degrade gracefully on thin or junk JDs.** Real-world JDs include vague templates and mislabeled sales roles. Below a match threshold the tool says "this JD gave me little to work with" rather than producing a confident-looking verdict from thin input.

**H. The career ladder problem and the "keep your reps" flag.** Bucket one work is how junior people build the judgment that bucket three requires. You develop crisis instincts by writing a hundred routine statements first. If the tool just says "automate the junior work," it's accidentally arguing for pulling up the ladder. Proposed fix: certain bucket one tasks get a "keep your reps" flag, meaning automatable AND foundational for skill-building. The tool's advice then varies by career stage. Early career: automate the volume, keep some reps, and use AI as a sparring partner (it critiques your draft) rather than a ghostwriter (it replaces your draft). Leaders: automate freely for yourself, but design your team's roles so junior people still get their reps.
